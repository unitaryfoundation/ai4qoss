# Bug report

Last verified: 2026-08-28
Status: draft

## Task

File a bug report that a maintainer can act on. Done means: a minimal reproducing test
or script, the observed and expected behavior, and the environment pin.

## Loop

1. Agent (or human with an assistant) reduces the failing case to a minimal script or
   test.
2. Runs it in a clean pinned environment against the latest development head to confirm
   it fails, and against the latest release to check whether it is already fixed.
3. Files the report with the reproduction attached and the disclosure footer.

## Verification

The reproduction itself: it must fail on the current head, in a clean environment,
deterministically (fixed seed for probabilistic behavior, with the tolerance stated).

- Cost: one clean-environment run.
- Where it can be fooled: environment-dependent failures that vanish in the
  maintainer's setup. The pin plus a container reference limits this.

## Guardrails

- Rule for the receiving repo: reports without a reproduction are not reviewed. State
  this in CONTRIBUTING and close non-conforming reports with a pointer to it.
- Reports generated by scanning tools without a run-confirmed reproduction are not
  accepted.
- Check the receiving project's policy before an agent files anything. Qiskit's
  contributing guide forbids an agent from posting issues, pull requests, or comments
  autonomously, and PennyLane's AI policy covers issues explicitly. Under those rules
  this harness runs with a human filing the report.

## Cost and results

We have not run this harness ourselves, and nothing published measures AI-assisted bug
reporting against quantum repositories: not volume, not acceptance rate, not the
maintainer time it consumes. No quantum project publishes counts of the AI-generated
issues it receives. The numbers below come from adjacent settings and bound what to
expect.

The reproduction step is the hard part. On 1,983 real GitHub issues, the best agent
produced a test that genuinely fails before the fix and passes after it in 18.5
percent of cases (SWT-Bench,
[arXiv:2406.12952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.12952)). LIBRO reproduces 251 of 750
Defects4J bugs, or 33 percent, and 32 percent of a 31-bug set postdating its model's
training data ([ICSE 2023](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSE48619.2023.00194)); the same
method scores 14.1 percent on SWT-Bench, so the higher figure describes Defects4J
rather than the method. An agent asked to file a report will therefore fail this card's
verification most of the time, which is the intended behavior: the loop reports that it
cannot reproduce rather than filing.

What an unfiltered stream of AI-assisted reports costs the receiving side is
documented in curl's decision to end its bug bounty on 31 January 2026. The share of
submissions that were confirmed vulnerabilities fell from above 15 percent over the
program's lifetime to below 5 percent in 2025, with roughly 20 percent of submissions
judged low-quality AI-generated reports, arriving at about two per week, each engaging
three or four people for 30 minutes and sometimes up to three hours
([Stenberg, July 2025](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2025/07/14/death-by-a-thousand-slops/),
[January 2026](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/01/26/the-end-of-the-curl-bug-bounty/)).
The trajectory did not stay there. By April 2026 Stenberg reported the confirmed rate
back at 15 to 16 percent on roughly double the volume, with almost every security
report now using AI to some degree
([April 2026](https://daniel.haxx.se/blog/2026/04/22/high-quality-chaos/)). The 2025
trough was not a steady state, and the problem it left behind is volume rather than a
falling hit rate.

Confirmation rates published by quantum bug-finding tools range from 10 to 100
percent, and the spread measures how much the authors filtered before filing rather
than how good the tools are. At one end, LintQ found 216 real bugs in Qiskit programs
and filed 70 of them, stating that reporting adequately is itself time-consuming; 7
were confirmed or fixed ([FSE 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3660802)). At the other,
KQFuzz filed 13 curated reports against Qiskit, PennyLane, and Cirq and had all 13
confirmed ([arXiv:2607.25647](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.25647)); Fuzz4All filed 11
Qiskit bugs with 8 confirmed
([arXiv:2308.04748](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.04748)). LintQ's numbers are the useful
ones for this card, because they price the step the card is about: finding candidates
is cheap, and turning one into a report a maintainer will act on is not.

That step carries most of the load in quantum software. Across 394 confirmed defects
in 12 quantum simulators, 78.4 percent were found through user reports against 10.7
percent through automated testing
([arXiv:2603.22789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.22789)).

What maintainers want from a report was already documented in 2008: among surveyed
developers, 83 percent ranked steps to reproduce among the most useful contents of a
report, and 79 percent ranked errors in those steps among the most severe problems,
while 10 percent ranked duplicates that way and none ranked spam that way
([Bettenburg et al., FSE 2008](https://doi.org/10.1145/1453101.1453146)). Those are
ratings of importance and severity rather than counts of what reports contain, and the
survey predates AI-assisted submissions, so the last figure says that spam was not
then a leading concern. The verification on this card is the mechanized version of the
first preference.

Reproductions also decay, which is why the environment pin belongs in the report. Of
37 Bugs4Q artifacts, 62.2 percent still reproduced on Qiskit 0.20.1 against 16.2
percent on 2.3.1, with 93.6 percent of the failures caused by dependencies
([arXiv:2606.27124](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27124)).
